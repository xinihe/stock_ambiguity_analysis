# Research Proposal: Global Geopolitical Risk, Ambiguity, and Emerging Markets

## Abstract

This research investigates the impact of global geopolitical risk (GPR) on the Chinese capital market, with a focus on disentangling the distinct roles of risk and ambiguity in asset pricing. While emerging markets are known to be sensitive to external shocks, the precise mechanisms through which geopolitical tensions transmit to financial assets remain under-explored. We argue that traditional risk-based models, which rely on quantifiable probabilities (i.e., volatility), are insufficient to capture the full spectrum of uncertainty generated by geopolitical events. Such events are often characterized by "Knightian uncertainty," or ambiguity, where the underlying probability distributions of outcomes are unknown. This study makes three primary contributions. First, we construct a daily measure of financial ambiguity for the Chinese market and demonstrate its significant explanatory power for stock returns, independent of conventional risk metrics. Our findings indicate that ambiguity exerts a negative pressure on returns, consistent with theories of ambiguity aversion. Second, we establish a direct empirical link between global geopolitical risk and financial ambiguity, showing that heightened GPR leads to a significant increase in market ambiguity. This suggests that ambiguity is a key channel through which geopolitical shocks are priced. Third, we develop an integrated model that shows both GPR and ambiguity have significant, negative impacts on returns, often diminishing the role of traditional risk measures. Our empirical strategy employs a combination of time-series analysis (VAR), event studies, and instrumental variable techniques to ensure robustness and address endogeneity concerns. By elevating ambiguity as a critical state variable, this research provides a more nuanced understanding of asset pricing in emerging markets and offers important insights for investors and policymakers navigating a world of heightened geopolitical uncertainty.

## Introduction

The global financial landscape of the twenty-first century is characterized by deep interconnectedness and the rapid transmission of shocks across borders. Within this environment, emerging economies, while offering significant growth opportunities, are particularly vulnerable to external global forces. Among the most potent of these forces is geopolitical risk—the threats and uncertainties arising from international conflicts, political instability, and diplomatic tensions. The intensification of geopolitical events over the last two decades, from trade disputes and military conflicts to sanctions and political crises, has created a climate of heightened uncertainty that reverberates through financial markets worldwide. This paper investigates the impact of global geopolitical risk (GPR) on the Chinese capital market, a critical and globally integrated emerging market. Our central thesis is that the effects of geopolitical risk are not monolithic; instead, they manifest through distinct channels of risk and ambiguity, two related but conceptually separate dimensions of uncertainty that have profound and differential implications for asset pricing.

The theoretical distinction between risk and ambiguity traces its origins to the foundational work of Frank Knight (1921) and the subsequent formalization in decision theory by Ellsberg (1961). Risk refers to situations where the probabilities of future outcomes are known or can be reliably estimated, allowing for quantification and management through conventional financial models. Volatility, the standard deviation of returns, is the canonical measure of risk in financial economics. Ambiguity, in contrast, refers to what is often termed "Knightian uncertainty"—a state where the underlying probability distributions of outcomes are themselves unknown or unstable. This form of uncertainty arises when information is scarce, unreliable, or conflicting, preventing rational agents from forming confident probabilistic beliefs. Geopolitical events are a prime source of such ambiguity. The outcomes of a trade war, the trajectory of a military conflict, or the stability of a political regime are inherently difficult to predict, and the probabilities of their various resolutions are often unknowable.

In the presence of ambiguity, standard expected utility theory may fail to capture investor behavior. Models of ambiguity aversion, such as the multiple-priors framework of Gilboa and Schmeidler (1989) or the smooth ambiguity model of Klibanoff, Marinacci, and Mukerji (2005), posit that investors are averse not only to risk but also to ambiguity itself. This aversion leads them to demand a premium for holding assets with uncertain payoff distributions, which can depress asset prices and expected returns. Despite a rich theoretical literature on ambiguity, its empirical application in finance has been limited, creating a significant gap in our understanding of asset pricing dynamics. Much of the empirical literature operationalizes uncertainty solely through the lens of risk (i.e., volatility), thereby conflating two distinct concepts and potentially misattributing the effects of ambiguity to risk, or overlooking them entirely.

This research addresses this empirical gap by making several primary contributions. To begin, we position ambiguity as an independent and measurable state variable with testable implications for asset prices in an emerging market context. We construct a daily measure of financial ambiguity for the Chinese market, which has been slightly modified to be less sensitive to hyperparameter choices (as detailed in Appendix A), and test its explanatory power for stock returns alongside traditional risk metrics. By doing so, we aim to disentangle the effects of risk and ambiguity, providing a more nuanced understanding of how uncertainty is priced. Our preliminary findings suggest that ambiguity is not only statistically significant but also economically meaningful, exerting a negative pressure on daily returns consistent with the predictions of ambiguity aversion theory.

Furthermore, we establish a direct empirical linkage between global geopolitical risk and financial ambiguity. While it is intuitive that geopolitical tensions should breed uncertainty, the precise channels through which this occurs are not well understood. We hypothesize that GPR acts as a powerful driver of ambiguity, as it undermines the stability of the assumptions upon which investors base their probabilistic forecasts. We test this hypothesis by examining the dynamic relationship between a widely used index of global geopolitical risk (the GPRD index of Caldara and Iacoviello, 2022) and our measure of ambiguity. This analysis moves beyond a simple correlation to explore potential causal pathways using time-series methods, such as vector autoregression (VAR) and Granger causality tests. Our preliminary results indicate a significant positive relationship, where increases in global geopolitical risk are followed by a rise in measured ambiguity in the Chinese market.

Finally, by integrating these two lines of inquiry, we develop a more complete model of how external geopolitical shocks are transmitted to an emerging market. We argue that GPR affects returns not only directly but also indirectly through its influence on ambiguity. Our combined model, which includes GPR, ambiguity, and risk as explanatory variables for daily returns, shows that both GPR and ambiguity have significant negative effects on returns, while the significance of conventional risk is diminished. This suggests that in times of geopolitical stress, ambiguity becomes a dominant channel through which uncertainty is priced, potentially crowding out the effects of market volatility. This finding has important implications for investors, policymakers, and financial stability. For investors, it highlights the need to look beyond volatility when assessing market uncertainty. For policymakers, it provides evidence of how global political events can translate into domestic financial instability, even in a market with capital controls like China.

To ensure the robustness of our findings, our empirical strategy will incorporate several extensions. We plan to conduct instrumental variable (IV) analysis to address potential endogeneity concerns, using exogenous geopolitical events (e.g., major international summits, the imposition of sanctions, or outbreak of conflicts) as instruments. Furthermore, we will employ event-study methodologies to analyze the market's reaction to specific geopolitical shocks, tracing the impulse responses of ambiguity and returns in the aftermath of these events. By providing a rigorous empirical analysis of the interplay between geopolitical risk, ambiguity, and asset returns in a major emerging market, this research aims to advance our understanding of how uncertainty is priced in a globalized world and to elevate ambiguity as a critical variable in the empirical asset pricing literature.

### Geopolitical Risk and Financial Markets

The study of geopolitical risk has a long, albeit sporadic, history in economics. Early work often focused on the economic consequences of major wars, but the systematic analysis of broader geopolitical tensions is a more recent phenomenon. A significant catalyst for this field was the development of quantifiable indices of geopolitical risk, most notably the Geopolitical Risk (GPR) Index of Caldara and Iacoviello (2022). This index, based on the frequency of newspaper articles discussing geopolitical tensions, has enabled a new wave of empirical research into the effects of GPR on a wide range of economic and financial variables.

While the link between GPR and financial outcomes is well-established, the precise channels through which these effects are transmitted are less understood. Our research contributes to this literature by proposing that ambiguity is a key, and heretofore under-appreciated, channel. ### Ambiguity Aversion and Asset Pricing

The theoretical distinction between risk and ambiguity has been a cornerstone of decision theory since the work of Knight (1921) and Ellsberg (1961). However, the empirical application of these concepts in finance has been slower to develop. The standard asset pricing paradigm, based on expected utility theory, assumes that investors can assign probabilities to all future states of the world. In this framework, uncertainty is synonymous with risk, which is typically measured by the volatility of returns.

A growing body of theoretical and experimental work challenges this assumption. Models of ambiguity aversion, such as the multiple-priors framework of Gilboa and Schmeidler (1989) and the smooth ambiguity model of Klibanoff, Marinacci, and Mukerji (2005), posit that investors are averse not only to risk but also to ambiguity itself. <mcreference link="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X18301831" index="7">7</mcreference> This aversion leads them to demand a premium for holding assets with uncertain payoff distributions, which can depress asset prices and expected returns.

The empirical literature on ambiguity and asset pricing is still in its early stages, but a number of studies have found evidence consistent with the predictions of ambiguity aversion theory. <mcreference link="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11238-012-9343-2" index="6">6</mcreference> <mcreference link="https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1673494" index="8">8</mcreference> For example, Brenner and Izhakian (2018) develop a measure of ambiguity based on the dispersion of analysts' forecasts and find that it is negatively priced in the cross-section of U.S. stock returns. Other studies have used different proxies for ambiguity, such as the volatility of volatility or the dispersion of survey-based expectations, and have also found that ambiguity is associated with lower returns.

Our research builds on this emerging literature by constructing a daily measure of ambiguity for the Chinese market and testing its explanatory power for stock returns. This leads to our second hypothesis:

**H2: Ambiguity is negatively associated with daily returns.**

Finally, we seek to integrate these two streams of literature by examining the combined impact of geopolitical risk, ambiguity, and traditional risk on asset returns. We hypothesize that GPR has both a direct and an indirect effect on returns, with the indirect effect operating through the ambiguity channel. This leads to our third and final hypothesis:

**H3: Global geopolitical risk (GPRD) is negatively associated with daily returns, even after controlling for ambiguity and risk.**

By testing these hypotheses, we aim to provide a more complete and nuanced understanding of how uncertainty is priced in a major emerging market.

### Data and Causal Strategy

Our empirical analysis relies on a rich dataset of daily financial and economic indicators for the Chinese market, spanning from [Start Date] to [End Date]. The core variables include:

*   **Market Returns:** We use the daily returns of the CSI 300 index as our primary measure of market performance.
*   **Ambiguity:** Our daily ambiguity measure is constructed following the methodology outlined in Appendix A. This measure is designed to be less sensitive to hyperparameter choices and captures the market's uncertainty about the underlying data-generating process.
*   **Risk:** We use a standard GARCH(1,1) model to estimate the daily conditional volatility of the CSI 300 returns, which serves as our primary measure of risk.
*   **Geopolitical Risk (GPR):** We employ the daily GPR index from Caldara and Iacoviello (2022). We use the overall GPR index, as well as its sub-indices for China and the United States, to capture both global and country-specific geopolitical tensions.

Our identification strategy is designed to move beyond simple correlations and establish a causal link between ambiguity, GPR, and market returns. We employ a multi-pronged approach:

1.  **Baseline Regressions:** We start with a series of baseline regressions to establish the statistical significance of the relationships between our key variables.
2.  **Instrumental Variables (IV):** To address potential endogeneity concerns, we use an instrumental variable approach.
3.  **Mediation and Moderation Analysis:** We examine the channels through which GPR affects returns by testing whether ambiguity acts as a mediator and whether market conditions moderate the impact of ambiguity.

### Control Variables

To isolate the effects of ambiguity and GPR, we will include the following standard control variables in our regressions:

*   **Risk-Free Rate:** The yield on the 3-month Chinese government bond.
    *   **Justification:** This represents the baseline return for a risk-free investment and is a fundamental component of asset pricing models.
*   **Term Spread:** The difference between the yields on 10-year and 3-month Chinese government bonds.
    *   **Justification:** This variable is a widely used proxy for the stance of monetary policy and market expectations of future economic growth.
*   **Credit Spread:** The difference between the yield on a high-yield Chinese corporate bond index and a government bond of similar maturity.
    *   **Justification:** This captures the market's perception of default risk and overall credit conditions, which are known to affect investor risk appetite.
*   **Market Liquidity:** We will use the Amihud illiquidity measure, calculated from daily trading volume and returns.
    *   **Justification:** Liquidity is a priced factor in asset returns. It is crucial to control for it, as periods of high ambiguity may also coincide with lower market liquidity.
*   **VIX Index:** The CBOE Volatility Index will be included to control for global risk aversion.
    *   **Justification:** The VIX, often called the "fear gauge," captures global investor sentiment and risk appetite. Controlling for it helps to ensure that our ambiguity measure is not simply proxying for global risk aversion.

### Appendix A: Ambiguity Measure Construction

Our daily ambiguity measure is constructed based on the framework of adaptive learning and model uncertainty, as proposed by [Author, Year]. The core idea is to capture the uncertainty that investors face when they are unsure about the true data-generating process (DGP) of asset returns. We assume that investors consider a set of plausible models to describe the return dynamics and update their beliefs about these models over time.

**1. Model Set:**

We define a set of $M$ competing models for the daily returns of the CSI 300 index. These models are all variations of an AR(1)-GARCH(1,1) process, but they differ in their parameter values. Specifically, we consider a grid of values for the key parameters of the GARCH model, which governs the volatility dynamics. This grid is centered around the parameter estimates obtained from a full-sample estimation of the AR(1)-GARCH(1,1) model.

Let $R_t$ be the daily return at time $t$. The general form of the models is:

$R_t = \mu + \phi R_{t-1} + \epsilon_t$
$\epsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t, \quad z_t \sim N(0,1)$
$\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha \epsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta \sigma_{t-1}^2$

The set of models is generated by allowing the parameters $(\omega, \alpha, \beta)$ to vary over a pre-defined range.

**2. Model Weighting and Ambiguity Calculation:**

We assume that investors use a recursive algorithm to update the weights assigned to each model in the set. The weight of model $i$ at time $t$, denoted by $w_{i,t}$, is updated based on its recent forecasting performance. We use a discounted least squares approach, where more recent errors are weighted more heavily.

The forecasting error of model $i$ at time $t$ is given by:

$e_{i,t} = R_t - \hat{R}_{i,t}$

where $\hat{R}_{i,t}$ is the one-step-ahead forecast of the return from model $i$.

The weight for each model is updated as follows:

$w_{i,t} \propto w_{i,t-1}^\delta \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\lambda} e_{i,t}^2\right)$

where $\delta \in (0,1)$ is a "forgetting factor" that discounts past performance, and $\lambda$ is a gain parameter. The weights are then normalized to sum to one.

Our measure of ambiguity at time $t$, $Amb_t$, is defined as the entropy of the distribution of model weights:

$Amb_t = -\sum_{i=1}^{M} w_{i,t} \ln(w_{i,t})$

A higher value of $Amb_t$ indicates greater ambiguity, as the weights are more evenly distributed across the set of models, implying that investors are more uncertain about which model is the best representation of the DGP.

**3. Robustness to Hyperparameters:**

A key feature of our approach is its robustness to the choice of hyperparameters, particularly the forgetting factor $\delta$. Instead of relying on a single value of $\delta$, we compute the ambiguity measure for a range of plausible values and then take the average. This approach ensures that our measure is not driven by an arbitrary choice of a single hyperparameter and provides a more stable and reliable indicator of market ambiguity.

This methodology provides a time-varying measure of ambiguity that captures the dynamic nature of model uncertainty in financial markets. It is grounded in the principles of adaptive learning and provides a theoretically sound and empirically operational way to quantify ambiguity.
